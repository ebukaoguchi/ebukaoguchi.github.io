I teach how computers work — from source code to silicon to the operating system — and build the labs, curricula, and communities that help students master systems.

I am a tenure-track Assistant Professor of Computer and Information Sciences at Spelman College. My research in wireless and physical-layer security is what keeps my systems teaching current; my classroom is where that research has to earn its keep, one cohort of students at a time.

Teaching

My courses follow one continuous pathway through the machine. Students meet each layer as a thing they can build, measure, and break, not as a diagram to memorise. Select a stage:

STAGE 01Source code

02

Educational Leadership

Teaching well in my own classroom is the floor, not the ceiling. These are the projects through which my teaching is meant to outlast my own course schedule: materials and structures other instructors can adopt.

Project

A shared lab repository for systems courses

Adaptable laboratory modules for computer organization, architecture, and operating systems, with staged milestones and verifiable checkpoints, published openly so colleagues can adopt, remix, and improve them. The collection is live and growing: eleven modules so far, from a first look inside QtSpim to a capstone in which students build their own MIPS assembler in C++. github.com/ebukaoguchi/systems-labs

Live
Project

The Cybersecurity Clinic, in the curriculum

As a collaborator on the Google.org Cybersecurity Clinic at Spelman, I work on integrating real, client-facing security work for under-resourced organisations into the undergraduate cybersecurity curriculum, service and coursework reinforcing each other.

Project

Developing TAs and peer mentors

Large systems courses run on their teaching teams. Planned: structured preparation and mentoring for the undergraduate TAs and peer mentors who staff labs and office hours, because students often learn the pathway first from someone one step ahead of them on it.

03

Scholarship of Teaching & Learning

My systems pathway generates its own research questions: about learning rather than about networks. Current lines of inquiry:

  • How do students build mental models of address translation and virtual memory, and where do those models first break?
  • Where do caching and memory-hierarchy misconceptions form, and which laboratory interventions actually dislodge them?
  • What do near-peer voices, students one cohort ahead, do for belonging and persistence in demanding systems courses?

Classroom studies run with ethics approval, and results, including negative ones, are shared with the computing-education community (SIGCSE, ITiCSE) so that other instructors can build on them.

04

Mentoring

Mentoring is where the pathway becomes personal. At Spelman I mentor undergraduate researchers through their first systems and security projects: framing a question, building the experiment, and presenting the result. I am a recipient of the Google CS Research Mentorship Program (CSRMP) and a member of the Institute for African American Mentoring in Computing Sciences (iAAMCS); the students I have mentored include a Ph.D. student at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln and undergraduate researchers at Spelman.

I was selected as Spelman’s faculty fellow for the ACS Teaching & Learning Workshop at Rollins College (2026), a programme devoted to evidence-based, inclusive teaching in liberal-arts settings.

05

The research behind the teaching

My technical research develops authentication, message-integrity, and trust-establishment mechanisms for emerging wireless and cyber-physical systems. It is the reason my systems courses can teach security as a property of every layer, and it continues alongside my teaching, through the NSF Center for Distributed Confidential Computing, where I am a collaborator.
