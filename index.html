Biography

I am an Assistant Professor of Computer Science at Spelman College in Atlanta, Georgia. My research lies at the intersection of wireless security, Internet of Things (IoT), and agricultural technology.

I received my Ph.D. in Computer Science and Engineering from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln in 2025, where I was advised by Prof. Nirnimesh Ghose. Prior to my doctoral studies, I earned my M.S. from Changchun University of Science and Technology, China, and my B.Eng from Nnamdi Azikiwe University, Nigeria.

My work focuses on securing critical infrastructure in challenging environments—specifically Agricultural IoT (Ag-IoT), underground sensor networks, and autonomous systems. I combine physical-layer security with lightweight cryptography to build resilient systems that protect our food supply and autonomous networks.

🎯 Current Focus

I am currently establishing my research group at Spelman focused on Secure Precision Agriculture. I am actively looking for motivated undergraduate students interested in IoT, Embedded Systems, and Cybersecurity.

Research Interests

My research secures the critical infrastructure of emerging wireless networks. I specialize in message integrity and authentication across diverse and challenging environments.

Agricultural IoT Security (Ag-IoT)

Goal: Establishing trust in underground sensor networks where physical access is difficult.
Method: I integrate physical layer channel features with lightweight cryptographic primitives to ensure data integrity without draining sensor batteries.
Supported by the National Science Foundation (NSF).

Autonomous Vehicle Security

Goal: Preventing adversarial takeover of autonomous systems (self-driving cars/drones).
Method: We utilize real-time trajectory and motion vector data to verify that incoming messages match the physical behavior of the vehicle, detecting spoofing attacks instantly.

Radio Frequency Fingerprinting

Goal: Identifying devices based on their unique hardware imperfections.
Method: Using Deep Learning to analyze signal characteristics in underground environments, creating unique "fingerprints" for authentication.
